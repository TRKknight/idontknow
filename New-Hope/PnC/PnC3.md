### **Rigor Mortis**
* **Characteristics:** A state of **contracture** that occurs several hours after death where all muscles of the body contract and become rigid.
* **Properties:** It occurs without the need for action potentials. The process happens more rapidly at **higher temperatures**.
* **Functions/Mechanism:** The rigidity is caused by the **loss of all ATP** from the muscle cells. ATP is required to cause the separation of the cross-bridges from actin filaments during the relaxation process; without it, the bridges remain attached.
* **Clinical Correlates:** The state of rigor lasts until **muscle proteins deteriorate** (approximately 15–25 hours later), a process resulting from autolysis caused by enzymes released from **lysosomes**.

### **SA Node (Sinoatrial Node)**
* **Characteristics:** Identified as the **primary pacemaker** of the heart. 
* **Properties:** It is characterized by **automaticity** and **rhythmicity**. It generates **slow response action potentials** (pacemaker potentials).
* **Functions:** It generates the cardiac impulse and **determines the rate** at which the heart beats.
* **Location:** Situated in the heart.
* **Regulation:** Its rate of discharge is controlled by the **autonomic nervous system**; parasympathetic stimulation (vagal) decreases the rate, while sympathetic stimulation increases it.

### **Saliva**
* **Characteristics:** A major gastrointestinal secretion characterized by **exclusive neural regulation**.
* **Properties:** It has a **pH of 7.5 to 8.0** and a daily volume of approximately **1.0–1.5 liters**.
* **Functions:** 
 * **Digestion:** Hydrolyzes starch into maltose and dextrin via **ptyalin**.
 * **Protection:** **Lysozyme** acts as an antibacterial agent, and **IgA** provides local immunity.
 * **Mechanical:** Lubricates food for swallowing, facilitates the mixing of food, and maintains **oral hygiene** by washing away pathogenic bacteria.
* **Location:** Secreted into the **oral cavity**.
* **Types:** Classified by the nature of secretion: **serous** (watery), **mucous** (viscous), or **mixed**.
* **Regulation:** Controlled by autonomic nerves. **Parasympathetic activation** is the primary stimulus for secretion. Secretion can be an **unconditioned reflex** (contact with food) or a **conditioned reflex** (smell or thought of food).
* **Clinical Correlates:** 
 * **Xerostomia:** Persistent dry mouth due to decreased secretion.
 * **Sialorrhea:** Persistent increase in secretion.
 * **Sjogren’s Syndrome:** An autoimmune disorder presenting with xerostomia and xerophthalmia.
 * **Sialolithiasis:** Formation of stones in the salivary ducts.

### **Saliva Composition**
* **Components:** 
 * **Enzymes:** Primarily **ptyalin** (α-amylase) and **lingual lipase**.
 * **Proteins:** Includes **mucin** (for lubrication), lysozyme, and IgA.
 * **Ions:** Contains various electrolytes.
* **Regulation (Modification):** Saliva is initially formed in the **acini**. Its final composition is determined by **duct epithelial cells**, which modify the ionic concentration as the fluid passes through the ducts.
* **Factors Affecting:** 
 * **Parasympathetic Stimulation:** Increases secretion rich in **enzymes and mucin**.
 * **Sympathetic Stimulation:** Causes vasoconstriction, which decreases saliva formation and makes the resulting secretion **thick**.
 * **Medications:** Clozapine and pilocarpine increase secretion; antihistamines, diuretics, and antidepressants decrease it.

### **Salivary Glands**
* **Characteristics:** These glands are part of the alimentary tract and are unique because their secretion is under **exclusive neural regulation**.
* **Functions:** They produce saliva which facilitates **mastication**, provides **oral hygiene** by washing away bacteria, contains **lysozyme and IgA** for infection protection, and initiates the digestion of carbohydrates via **ptyalin**.
* **Location:** Found in and around the mouth cavity.
* **Components:** Anatomically composed of **acini** (sac-like dilations) and **ducts** lined with epithelial cells that modify the ionic composition of the final secretion.
* **Types:** 
 * **Major Glands:** Three pairs—**parotid, sublingual, and submandibular**.
 * **Minor Glands:** Located in the mucosa of the oral cavity, pharyngeal outlet, palates, and buccal pouches.
 * **By Secretion Nature:** **Serous** (watery), **mucous** (viscous), or **mixed** (moderately viscous).
* **Regulation:** 
 * **Parasympathetic:** The primary stimulus; activation (centers in the medulla) increases secretion rich in enzymes and mucin.
 * **Sympathetic:** Causes a transient increase followed by a decrease due to **vasoconstriction**, making the saliva thick.
 * **Reflexes:** Includes **unconditioned reflexes** (contact with food) and **conditioned reflexes** (smell or thought of food).
* **Factors Affecting:** 
 * **Increase:** Parasympathetic stimulation, Clozapine, and pilocarpine.
 * **Decrease:** Sympathetic stimulation (during acute stress), dehydration, and drugs like antihistamines, antidepressants, and atropine. 
 * **Aldosterone:** Increases sodium reabsorption from salivary ducts.
* **Clinical Correlates:** 
 * **Xerostomia:** Persistent dry mouth.
 * **Sjogren’s Syndrome:** Autoimmune disorder causing dry mouth and eyes.
 * **Sialorrhea:** Persistent increase in secretion (drooling).
 * **Sialolithiasis:** Formation of stones in salivary ducts.
 * **Frey Syndrome:** Sweating while eating due to misdirected nerve regeneration after parotid injury.

### **Secretory Juice Phases**
* **Characteristics:** Gastrointestinal (GI) secretion is divided into three distinct phases based on where the stimulus originates: **Cephalic, Gastric, and Intestinal**.
* **Functions:** To coordinate the release of digestive juices (saliva, gastric, pancreatic, and intestinal) with the arrival and movement of food.
* **Regulation:** 
 * **Cephalic Phase:** Predominantly regulated by **neural mechanisms** (e.g., thought, smell, or taste of food) via the vagus nerve. It accounts for a significant portion of salivary and some gastric secretion.
 * **Gastric Phase:** Regulated by both **hormonal (gastrin) and neural** mechanisms initiated by food entering the stomach.
 * **Intestinal Phase:** Regulated by both hormonal (e.g., **CCK and secretin**) and neural mechanisms as chyme enters the duodenum.

### **Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)**
* **Characteristics:** A model of a **nonspecific response** of the body to variety of "nocuous agents" or stressors.
* **Properties:** Focuses on the role of the **adrenal glands** and steroids in the stress response.
* **Types (Stages):**
 1. **Alarm:** The stressor disrupts homeostasis.
 2. **Resistance:** The body adjusted responses to adapt to the stress.
 3. **Exhaustion:** Continued stress leads to tissue damage, disease, and death.
* **Regulation:** Involves the coordination of the **sympathetic nervous system** and the **adrenal gland** (HPA axis) to restore homeostasis (allostasis).
* **Factors Affecting:** Triggered by stressors such as extreme cold, excessive exercise, drugs, or spinal shock.
* **Clinical Correlates:**
 * **Stress Triad:** Demonstrated by Selye as (i) **adrenal enlargement**, (ii) **atrophy of the thymus and lymph nodes**, and (iii) **hemorrhagic gastric ulcers**.
 * **Maladaptation:** Chronic stress can lead to **insulin resistance**, obesity, and suppression of immunity.

### **Sensory Receptors**
* **Characteristics:** These are specialized structures that detect the state of the body or the surroundings. They exhibit **differential sensitivities**, only responding to their **adequate stimulus**.
* **Properties:** They produce **graded responses** (receptor potentials) and are often **adaptable**, meaning their response decreases over time to a continuous stimulus.
* **Functions:** Act as **transducers**, changing various forms of sensory stimuli (mechanical, chemical, thermal, etc.) into nerve signals (action potentials).
* **Location:** Distributed throughout the body in the skin, internal organs, joints, and special sense organs (eyes, ears).
* **Types:** 
 * **By Location:** **Exteroceptors** (external environment/skin), **interoceptors** (visceral), and **proprioceptors** (body position).
 * **By Stimulus:** **Mechanoreceptors** (touch/pressure), **thermoreceptors** (temperature), **nociceptors** (pain), **chemoreceptors** (taste/smell/osmotic), and **electromagnetic receptors** (light).
* **Regulation:** Regulated by **adaptation** (increasing threshold to a stimulus) and **recruitment** (activation of additional receptors as stimulus intensity increases).
* **Clinical Correlates:** Loss of receptor function leads to specific deficits, such as the **Romberg sign** (inability to maintain balance with eyes closed due to loss of proprioception).

### **Sensory Transmission**
* **Characteristics/Properties:** Utilizes the **labeled line principle**, where nerve fibers project to specific ordered areas of the cortex.
* **Functions:** To convey information about the **modality, intensity, and location** of a stimulus to the central nervous system (CNS).
* **Components/Pathways:**
 * **Dorsal Column-Medial Lemniscal System:** Carries critical, high-fidelity tactile and position signals.
 * **Anterolateral System:** Carries less critical signals such as pain, temperature, and crude touch.
* **Regulation (Intensity Coding):** Intensity is transmitted via **spatial summation** (recruiting more fibers) and **temporal summation** (increasing the frequency of nerve impulses).
* **Factors Affecting:** Transmission can be impaired by nerve **axotomy, demyelination, ischemia, and compression**.
* **Clinical Correlates:** Defects in specific pathways result in characteristic losses, such as **astereognosis** (inability to recognize objects by touch) or abnormalities in pain perception.

* **Characteristics:** Transmission occurs along both **myelinated and unmyelinated fibers**. Myelinated fibers utilize **saltatory conduction**, where the impulse "jumps" from one node of Ranvier to the next.
* **Properties:**

 * **Sarcopenia:** The loss of muscle mass and function associated with aging.
 * **Rigor Mortis:** Muscle rigidity following death due to ATP depletion.

### **Skeletal Muscle Contraction**
* **Characteristics:** Initiated by the discharge of a motor neuron. It is capable of **rapid force development and shortening** and can maintain contractile force for relatively long periods. Approximately 40% of the body is composed of skeletal muscle.
* **Properties:** Includes **summation of contraction, staircase phenomenon, tetanization, and post-tetanic potentiation**. It is governed by the **length-tension relationship** (Starling law) and the **load-velocity relationship**.
* **Functions:** Facilitates body movement, maintains **posture** (via extensors), and enables protective **withdrawal reflexes** (via flexors).
* **Location:** Primarily **attached to the skeleton** (bones).
* **Components:** Organized into muscle bundles (fascicles) containing cylindrical fibers. Key structural elements include **myofibrils (actin and myosin filaments)**, the **sarcomere** (functional unit), and the **sarcotubular system** (T-tubules and sarcoplasmic reticulum). Regulatory proteins include **tropomyosin and troponin**.
* **Types:** Classified mechanically as **isometric** (length does not change) or **isotonic** (tension remains unchanged while length changes).
* **Regulation:** Controlled via **excitation-contraction coupling**, where a surface action potential triggers the release of **calcium ions (Ca⁺⁺)** from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the sarcoplasm.
* **Factors Affecting:**
 * **Force:** Proportional to initial fiber length and the number of active motor units.
 * **Velocity:** Shortening velocity is **inversely proportional** to the load.
 * **Metabolism:** Influenced by the availability of nutrients (oxygen, glucose) and the accumulation of metabolites like **lactic acid**.
* **Clinical Correlates:**
 * **Muscle Fatigue:** The decrease in tension due to previous contractile activity.
 * **Rigor Mortis:** Muscle rigidity after death due to ATP loss.
 * **Disorders:** Includes **Myasthenia Gravis** (acetylcholine receptor destruction), **Muscular Dystrophy** (dystrophin gene mutations), and **Muscle Cramps** (ECF electrolyte imbalances).

### **Sleep Apnea Syndrome**
* **Characteristics:** A sleep disorder characterized by the **periodic cessation of breathing** during sleep.
* **Types:**
 * **Obstructive:** Caused by the physical blockage of the upper airways.
 * **Central:** Resulting from a failure of the brain's respiratory centers to send signals to the breathing muscles.
* **Clinical Correlates:** There is a strong interaction between **obesity** and obstructive sleep apnea. It is categorized alongside other major sleep disorders like narcolepsy and insomnia .

### **Slow Waves (Basal Electrical Rhythm - BER)**
* **Characteristics:** Slow, undulating changes in the **resting membrane potential** of gastrointestinal smooth muscle.
* **Properties:** They are **not action potentials** themselves but determine the rhythm of gastrointestinal contractions.
* **Functions:** Act as the **pacemaker** for the gut, setting the maximum frequency for spike potentials and subsequent motility.
* **Location:** Found in the **smooth muscle of the gastrointestinal tract**, particularly in the stomach and small intestine.
* **Regulation:** Generated by specialized cells known as **Interstitial cells of Cajal** .

### **Slow and Fast Skeletal Muscle Fibers**
* **Characteristics:** 
 * **Slow (Type I):** Also called **red muscle**; fibers are smaller and have a more extensive blood supply and higher myoglobin content.
 * **Fast (Type II):** Also called **white muscle**; fibers are larger and adapted for rapid power.
* **Properties:**
 * **Slow:** Fatigue-resistant, low-force generation, and slow conduction velocity.
 * **Fast:** High-force generation, **fatigable**, and fast conduction velocity.
* **Components:** Fast fibers have an extensive sarcoplasmic reticulum for rapid Ca⁺⁺ release and high **myosin ATPase activity**. Slow fibers contain significantly more **mitochondria**.
* **Regulation (Size Principle):** Smaller motoneurons innervate slow motor units, while larger, fast-conducting motoneurons innervate fast motor units.
* **Factors Affecting:** Distribution is partly determined by **genetics**. Regular **isometric exercise** increases the number of slow fibers, while **isotonic exercise** increases the percentage of fast fibers.

### **Small-Molecule, Rapidly Acting Neurotransmitters**
* **Characteristics:** A group of synaptic transmitters characterized by **small molecular size** and **rapid action**.
* **Properties:** They cause most **acute responses** of the nervous system, such as the immediate transmission of sensory and motor signals.
* **Functions:** Mediate rapid signaling across synapses in the brain and peripheral nervous system.
* **Types:** 
 * **Class I:** Acetylcholine.
 * **Class II (Amines):** Norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, serotonin, and histamine.
 * **Class III (Amino Acids):** GABA, glycine, glutamate, and aspartate.
 * **Class IV:** **Nitric Oxide (NO)**.
* **Location:** Secreted by various neurons throughout the nervous system, including motor neurons at the **neuromuscular junction** (Acetylcholine).
* **Regulation:** Synthesized in the **cytosol of the presynaptic terminal** and absorbed into transmitter vesicles [History]. Release is triggered by the influx of calcium during an action potential.
* **Clinical Correlates:** Imbalances are linked to several disorders:
 * **Dopamine:** Linked to **Schizophrenia** and Parkinson’s disease.
 * **Serotonin and Norepinephrine:** Associated with **clinical depression**.
 * **Acetylcholine:** Deficiency is associated with **Alzheimer’s disease**.

### **Smooth Muscle**
* **Characteristics:** Also called **"plain" muscles**, they lack the regular pattern of cross-striations found in skeletal and cardiac muscle. Cells are small (100–300 µm long, 5–10 µm diameter) and **spindle-shaped** with a single, elongated, centrally placed nucleus.
* **Properties:** 
 * **Mechanical:** They exhibit **plasticity** (the length-tension relationship is flexible and non-linear). They can remain contracted for long periods with **low energy expenditure**, endowing them with superior metabolic economy.
 * **Electrical:** They show **spontaneous electrical activity** and are **functional syncytia** (impulse spreads from cell to cell). Action potential duration is long (hundreds of milliseconds) and has a variable speed of conduction. 
 * **Resilience:** They are generally **resistant to fatigue** and cannot be tetanized.
* **Functions:** 
 * **Regulation of Flow:** In vessels and airways, contraction narrows the passage to reduce flow; in sphincters, it can totally block flow. 
 * **Motility:** In the GI tract, inner circular and outer longitudinal layers promote the mixing and forward movement of food. 
 * **Expulsion:** In the uterus and urinary bladder, contraction expels contents when the organ is stretched.
* **Location:** Found in the walls of **hollow viscera** (intestine, bronchi, uterus, ureters), **blood vessels**, the **iris** of the eye, and **pilomotor muscles** of the skin.
* **Components:** Contains thick (myosin), thin (actin, tropomyosin), and intermediate filaments. It **lacks troponin**, using the calcium-binding protein **calmodulin** instead. It lacks an organized T tubular system but contains **caveolae** to increase surface area.
* **Types:** 
 * **Unitary (Single-unit):** Found in hollowed viscera; acts as a single unit.
 * **Multiunit:** Found in the iris, pilomotor muscles, and some blood vessels; fibers act independently.
* **Regulation:** 
 * **Nervous:** Regulated by branches of the **autonomic nervous system** (ANS).
 * **Molecular:** Contraction is triggered by an increase in intracellular **Ca²⁺**, which binds to **calmodulin**. This complex activates **myosin light chain kinase (MLCK)**, which phosphorylates the myosin light chain to allow binding with actin. 
* **Factors Affecting:** Stimulated by hormones such as **Angiotensin II**, vasopressin, and **endothelin**; inhibited (relaxed) by **Adenosine** via increased cAMP. Physical **stretch** also triggers contraction in unitary smooth muscle.

### **Somnambulism**
* **Clinical Correlates:** Categorized as a major **sleep disorder** in clinical assessments.

### **Spectrin and Ankyrin**
* **Characteristics/Properties:** These are specialized **peripheral proteins** of the red blood cell (RBC) membrane. Spectrin is a fibrous protein; Ankyrin has two domains, one binding to spectrin and the other to band-3 protein.
* **Functions:** They are essential for the **stabilization of the RBC membrane** and maintaining the cell's unique **biconcave shape**. Ankyrin specifically maintains membrane integrity.
* **Location:** Situated on the **inner surface** of the red cell membrane, linked to the cytoskeleton.
* **Clinical Correlates:** Defects or deficiencies in these proteins lead to **hereditary spherocytosis** and **elliptocytosis**. These defects make RBCs rigid, leading to their premature destruction in the spleen.

### **Sperm (Spermatozoa)**
* **Characteristics:** A matured, motile cell rich in DNA. It is approximately **65 µm long** with an average diameter of 2 µm.
* **Properties:** Motility is its hallmark, with a velocity of approximately **1–3 mm/min**.
* **Functions:** To penetrate and fertilize the **ovum** to deliver paternal genetic information.
* **Location:** Formed in the **seminiferous tubules** of the testes and stored in the **epididymis**.
* **Components:** 
 * **Head:** Contains the condensed nucleus and the **acrosome**, a cap-like vesicle filled with proteolytic enzymes (hyaluronidase, acrosin) for ovum penetration.
 * **Tail (Flagellum):** Composed of a skeleton of 11 microtubules (**axoneme**) and a collection of **mitochondria** in the "middle piece" that provide ATP for movement.
* **Types:** Classified as **male sperm** (carrying the Y chromosome) or **female sperm** (carrying the X chromosome).
* **Regulation:** Spermatogenesis is stimulated by hormones (testosterone, FSH, LH) and regulated by negative feedback from **inhibin**.
* **Factors Affecting:** 
 * **Temperature:** Normal production requires a temperature lower than body temperature; the **cremaster and dartos muscles** adjust the proximity of the testes to the body to regulate this. 
 * **Capacitation:** Further maturation occurs only after ejaculation into the female tract, which increases motility.
* **Clinical Correlates:** **Azoospermia** (absence of sperm in ejaculate), **oligospermia** (low count), and infertility due to abnormal morphology or motility.

### **Spinal Cord**
* **Characteristics:** The lowest level of the central nervous system for the integration of motor control. 
* **Properties/Functions:** 
 * **Reflex Integration:** Serves as the primary center for **spinal reflexes** (stretch, inverse stretch, and withdrawal reflexes) necessary for posture and locomotion.
 * **Pattern Generation:** Contains neural circuitry that acts as a **pattern generator** for basic movements like walking.
 * **Visceral Control:** Integrates autonomic functions such as the **defecation reflex** and the **male sexual act**.
* **Location:** Situated within the vertebral canal, extending from the medulla aborally.
* **Components:** Consists of **spinal laminae**, a central motoneuron pool, and **spinal interneurons**.
* **Regulation:** Motoneurons are regulated by the **labeled line principle** through afferent inputs, and the **gamma motor neuron** activity is primarily influenced by descending fibers from the brain. 
* **Clinical Correlates:** 
 * **Transection:** Complete transection results in **spinal shock**, characterized by the initial loss of all reflexes. 
 * **Diseases:** Includes **Tabes dorsalis**, **Syringomyelia**, and **Anterior Myelopathy**. 
 * **Paralysis:** Lesions can result in Upper or Lower Motor Neuron paralysis, such as **hemiplegia**.

## Batch 7: Stress Responses, Sustentacular Cells, Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Function, Synapses, Synaptic Transmission

### **Stress Responses**
* **Characteristics:** Stress triggers a **nonspecific response** in the body, which can be acute (rapid onset, short-lived), subacute, or chronic (sustained for months or years). 
* **Properties:** The body perceives and responds to stress differently depending on the nature of the stressor; **regional sympathetic activation** occurs based on the stimulus—for example, mental stress increases cardiac sympathetic activity, while obesity enhances renal activity.
* **Functions:** The response is designed to provide extra activation of the body for **survival or adaptation** during strenuous activity or emergency situations.
* **Components:** 
 * **Perception:** Psychological stressors are processed by the **prefrontal cortex and limbic system**, while physical stressors involve the **brainstem and hypothalamus**.
 * **Effector Systems:** These include the **Sympathetic Neural–Adrenal Medullary (SAM) axis** (rapid response) and the **Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Adrenal (HPA) axis** (slow, sustained response).
* **Types:** Stressors are categorized as **Physical** (e.g., pain, infection), **Psychological** (e.g., internal drives), or **Social** (e.g., divorce, unemployment). 
* **Clinical Correlates:** **Sympathovagal imbalance** caused by chronic stress is a major contributor to **metabolic syndrome**, including obesity, diabetes, and hypertension. Chronic stress is also associated with **PTSD, clinical depression**, and suppression of immune function.

### **Sustentacular Cells**

* **Characteristics:** These two divisions work reciprocally to regulate **visceral functions** and maintain homeostasis.
* **Properties:** 
 * **Sympathetic:** Known as the **adrenergic system**; features a high divergence ratio (1:100), leading to **widespread "mass" responses**.
 * **Parasympathetic:** Known as the **cholinergic system**; features a low divergence ratio (1:1 to 1:15), leading to **localized responses**.
* **Functions:** 
 * **Sympathetic:** Facilitates the **"fight-or-flight" response**, increases metabolic rate, heart rate, and blood glucose, and diverts blood to skeletal muscles.
 * **Parasympathetic:** Primarily focused on the **restoration and storage of energy**; it stimulates digestion and gastrointestinal secretion.

* **Characteristics:** Specialized connections between neurons for the **transmission of nerve impulses**. Each neuron forms an average of **2,000 synapses**.

* **Properties:** Exhibits characteristics such as **convergence, divergence, spatial and temporal summation**, and **synaptic delay** (approximately 0.5 ms).

* **Clinical Correlates:** **Synaptic plasticity** (long-term potentiation) is the fundamental process underlying **learning and memory**. Imbalances in specific neurotransmitters are associated with **depression** (norepinephrine/serotonin) and **schizophrenia** (dopamine).

### **Synaptic Transmitters**
* **Characteristics:** These are chemical substances (also known as transmitter substances or neurotransmitters) secreted by neurons at their nerve endings. More than 50 chemical substances have been identified or postulated to function as synaptic transmitters.
* **Properties:** They are broadly divided into two groups: **small-molecule, rapidly acting transmitters** and **neuropeptides** (slowly acting) of much larger molecular size.
* **Functions:** 
 * **General:** They act on receptor proteins in the postsynaptic membrane to excite, inhibit, or modify the sensitivity of the next neuron.
 * **Small-Molecule:** Cause most **acute responses** of the nervous system, such as transmitting sensory signals to the brain and motor signals back to muscles.
 * **Neuropeptides:** Cause **prolonged actions**, such as long-term changes in receptor numbers, long-term opening/closure of ion channels, or changes in synapse size and number.
* **Location:** Released by the **axon terminals** of neurons into **synaptic junctions**.
* **Types:** 
 * **Small-Molecule:** Categorized into four classes: **Class I** (Acetylcholine); **Class II** (Amines such as Norepinephrine, Epinephrine, Dopamine, Serotonin, Histamine); **Class III** (Amino acids like GABA, Glycine, Glutamate, Aspartate); and **Class IV** (Nitric Oxide).
 * **Neuropeptides/Growth Factors:** Categorized into **Hypothalamic-releasing hormones**, **Pituitary peptides**, **Peptides acting on gut and brain** (e.g., Substance P, Insulin, Glucagon), and peptides from other tissues (e.g., Angiotensin II, Bradykinin).
* **Regulation:** Released into the synaptic cleft where they bind to specific receptors. Some transmitter vesicles may be transported back to the cell body to provide a **feedback signal** for further synthesis.
* **Clinical Correlates:** Imbalances are linked to various disorders, such as **Glutamate excitotoxicity** in stroke and **Schizophrenia**.

### **Synaptic Vesicles**
* **Characteristics:** These are numerous vesicles located in the presynaptic terminal that contain neurotransmitters.
* **Functions:** To **store and release** neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft during signal transmission.
* **Location:** Situated within the **presynaptic terminal** (terminal button/synaptic knob).
* **Components:** Contain neurotransmitters and are clustered around specific points called **active zones**.
* **Types:** 
 * **Small vesicles with clear-core:** Contain Acetylcholine, GABA, Glycine, or Glutamate.
 * **Small vesicles with dense-core:** Contain Catecholamines.
 * **Large vesicles with dense-core:** Contain Neuropeptides.
* **Regulation:** Release is triggered by the influx of **calcium ions (Ca²⁺)** through voltage-gated channels located at the active zones.
* **Factors Affecting:** **Habituation** involves a decreased number of vesicles in the presynaptic terminal in response to repeated stimulation.

### **T-helper Cells**
* **Characteristics:** A major group of T lymphocytes (T cells) identified as T₄ cells or CD4+ cells.
* **Properties:** They possess receptor molecules (TCR) on their surfaces, most of which are **\alpha\beta TCRs**.
* **Functions:** They are essential for the **induction of both cellular and humoral immunities**.
* **Location:** Circulate in the blood and are present in lymphoid tissues.
* **Regulation:** Their multiplication is controlled by the **thymic humoral factor**.
* **Clinical Correlates:** **HIV** is a retrovirus that binds to the **CD4 protein** on the surface of T-helper cells, decreasing their numbers and causing **Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS)**.

### **T-helper Cell Subsets**
* **Types:** Primarily classified into **Type 1 (T_H1)** and **Type 2 (T_H2)** helper cells.

 * **T_H1 Cells:** Stimulate **cellular immunity**, NK cells, and macrophages by secreting cytokines like **IL-2** and **interferon-γ (IFN\gamma)**. They also produce TNF\beta.

* **Regulation:** 
 * **IL-12** (from macrophages/B cells) activates the development of T_H1 cells.
 * **IFN\gamma** secreted by T_H1 cells **inhibits** the activity of T_H2 cells.

### **TRP Family of Ion Channels**
* **Characteristics:** The **Transient Receptor Potential (TRP)** subfamily refers to a group of ion channels that serve as specialized temperature receptors.

 * **VR1 (Vanilloid receptor 1):** Respond to **very high temperatures** (nociceptive thermoceptors).
 * **VRL1 (Vanilloid receptor-like 1):** Respond to **moderate to high temperatures**.
* **Regulation:** 
 * **VR1** has a **PIP2 binding site**; its sensitivity increases when the quantity of bound PIP2 decreases.

* **Factors Affecting:** Cool receptors respond to ranges from 10–40°C, while warm receptors respond from 30–50°C. Sensitivity to high heat is increased by the presence of vanilloid compounds or reduced PIP2 binding.

### **Tactile Receptors**
* **Characteristics:** These are **mechanoreceptors** specialized for detecting physical changes such as touch, pressure, and vibration. 
* **Properties:** They exhibit varying adaptation rates; for example, touch receptors like Meissner's corpuscles are **rapidly adapting**, while others like Merkel discs are **slowly adapting**.

* **Types:** 
 * **Free nerve endings:** Detect touch and pressure; found everywhere in the skin.
 * **Meissner’s corpuscles:** Large, encapsulated, rapidly adapting receptors in hairless skin for fine touch.
 * **Merkel discs (Expanded tip receptors):** Grouped into **Iggo dome receptors**, they provide steady-state signals for continuous touch.
 * **Pacinian corpuscles:** Large, onion-like structures that detect **high-frequency vibration** and deep pressure.

### **Taste Cells**
* **Characteristics:** These are **modified epithelial cells** that act as the actual sensory receptors for taste. 
* **Properties:** They have a short life span of approximately **10 days** and are continually replaced through the mitotic division of surrounding epithelial cells.
* **Functions:** They function as **transducers**, converting chemical stimuli from food into electrical signals (receptor potentials) for the nervous system.
* **Location:** Situated within **taste buds** on the tongue (papillae), palate, pharynx, and proximal esophagus. They are the most numerous cells in a taste bud, making up 50–75% of the total.
* **Components:** Elongated cells with **microvilli (taste hairs)** that protrude into a **taste pore** to contact dissolved chemicals.

* **Characteristics:** Categorized as a **visceral sense** (gustation) mediated by chemoreceptors.
* **Functions:** To identify chemical qualities of food. Stimulation triggers **taste-salivary reflexes** and prepares the GI tract for digestion by activating gastric and pancreatic secretions.
* **Types (Primary Sensations):** There are five general categories: **sour, salty, sweet, bitter, and umami** (sensation of glutamate).
* **Regulation (Transduction):** 
 * **Salty and Sour:** Mediated by **ion channels** (e.g., epithelial Na+ channels for salt).
 * **Sweet, Bitter, and Umami:** Mediated by **G-protein coupled receptors** (e.g., gustducin for bitter).
* **Factors Affecting:** 
 * **Age:** Sensitivity decreases beyond age 45 due to the degeneration of taste buds.
 * **Nutritional State:** Salt deficiency creates a preference for salty food; calorie deficiency increases sweet preference.
 * **Drugs:** Substances like captopril or penicillamine can cause temporary taste loss.
* **Clinical Correlates:** 
 * **Ageusia:** Total absence of taste.

* **Location:** Located on the surface of the **Organ of Corti** within the cochlear duct of the inner ear.
* **Components:** Contains a **gel coating** that envelops the projecting hairs of the hair cells.

### **Teniae Coli and Haustra**

* **Components:**
 * **Teniae Coli:** Three distinct bands of **longitudinal smooth muscle** that run along the surface of the colon.
 * **Haustra:** Large, bag-like sacculations or bulges of the colonic wall.
* **Functions:** **Haustral contractions** serve as mixing movements that "dig" into and turn over fecal matter, facilitating the **absorption of water and electrolytes** by ensuring all contents contact the intestinal mucosa.

* **Regulation:** Primarily controlled by the **enteric nervous system** and the autonomic nervous system. Contraction is mediated by the **calcium-calmodulin system**.
* **Factors Affecting:** 
 * **Ions:** An increase in **calcium** stimulates contraction (vasoconstriction), while increased **potassium** inhibits it (vasodilation).
 * **Physical:** Responds to **stretch** of the organ wall [History].
* **Clinical Correlates:** Disorders include **achalasia** (failure of LES to relax), **Hirschsprung's disease** (congenital megacolon), and **Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)**.

### **Visual Field Defects**
* **Characteristics:** Abnormalities or blind spots (scotomata) within the normal physical space visible to an eye.
* **Types:** 
 * **Bitemporal hemianopia:** Loss of vision in both temporal fields.
 * **Scotomata:** Small localized blind spots.
 * **Word blindness:** A defect where visual images of words cannot be processed.
* **Clinical Correlates:** Defects are used to localize **lesions in the visual pathway**, such as the optic chiasm or optic radiation. They are helpful in diagnosing conditions like **retinitis pigmentosa**, glaucoma, and optic neuritis.

### **Voltage-Gated Sodium and Potassium Channels**
* **Characteristics:** These are specialized membrane proteins that open or close in response to changes in the **transmembrane potential**.
* **Properties:** 
 * **Sodium (Na+):** Characterized by rapid activation and a **positive-feedback cycle** during depolarization; they also feature a rapid inactivation gate.
 * **Potassium (K+):** Feature slower activation than sodium channels and are responsible for cell **repolarization**.
* **Functions:** They are the fundamental components responsible for the **initiation and propagation of action potentials** in excitable tissues.
* **Location:** Situated within the cell membranes of **neurons and muscle fibers**. In myelinated nerves, they are concentrated at the **nodes of Ranvier**.
* **Components:** Composed of protein subunits with specific **activation and inactivation gates**.
* **Regulation:** Controlled by voltage; however, they can be blocked by **local anesthetics** (like procaine) which stabilize the activation gates. **Calcium ion concentration** also regulates the threshold for sodium channel activation.
* **Clinical Correlates:** 
 * **Channelopathies:** General group of disorders caused by channel dysfunction.
 * **Long QT Syndrome:** Caused by mutations in K+ channel proteins (e.g., **KVLQT1**), leading to arrhythmias and deafness.
 * **Anesthesia:** Targeted by drugs to prevent the transmission of pain signals.

### **White Blood Cells (Leucocytes)**
* **Characteristics:** They are identified as the mobile units of the body's protective system. They perform mainly the defense functions of the body.
* **Properties:**
 * **Count:** The average concentration in an adult is approximately **7,000 per microliter of blood**.
 * **Life Span:** Their life span is variable depending on the specific cell type.
 * **Behavior:** They are capable of **endothelial adhesion, chemotaxis, and phagocytosis**.
* **Functions:**
 * **Primary Defense:** They protect the body against infectious agents such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites.
 * **Phagocytosis:** Neutrophils and monocytes (which become macrophages) kill invaders by engulfing and digesting them.
 * **Immunity:** Lymphocytes (T and B cells) are responsible for cellular and humoral immunity, respectively.
 * **Regulation:** Monocytes act as **immunoregulatory and antigen-presenting cells**.
 * **Tissue Repair:** Basophils take part in the initiation of tissue repair following an injury.
* **Location:** 
 * **Production:** Formed primarily in the **bone marrow** (myeloid series: granulocytes, monocytes) and **lymphoid tissues** (lymphocytes, plasma cells).
 * **Presence:** They circulate in the blood and are widely distributed throughout various tissues.
* **Components:** 
 * **Granulocytes:** Contain distinct cytoplasmic granules.
 * **Granule Contents:** These include **antibacterial compounds** in neutrophils and substances for interaction with T lymphocytes in allergy in eosinophils.
* **Types:** 
 * **Granulocytes:** Includes **Neutrophils** (50–70%), **Eosinophils** (1–4%), and **Basophils** (<0.5%).
 * **Agranulocytes:** Includes **Lymphocytes** (20–40%) and **Monocytes** (2–8%).
* **Regulation:**
 * **Leucopoiesis:** The process of formation is controlled by various factors.
 * **Chemical Stimuli:** Regulated by cytokines, **interleukins**, and Granulocyte-Monocyte Colony-Stimulating Factor (**GM-CSF**).
* **Factors Affecting:**
 * **Physiological Increase:** Stimulated by exercise, digestive processes, pregnancy, and **stress** (linked to catecholamines).
 * **Pathological Increase:** Caused by bacterial, viral, and fungal infections, allergies, and helminthic infestations.
* **Clinical Correlates:**
 * **Leukemia:** A disorder characterized by the uncontrolled production of abnormal white blood cells.
 * **Leukopenia:** A clinical state where the total white cell count is pathologically low.
 * **Inflammation:** Acute inflammation is marked by a large number of neutrophils, while chronic inflammation features a preponderance of lymphocytes.
 * **AIDS:** A condition resulting in the loss of specific immunity provided by T-helper cells.

* **Location:** The primary tumor is typically situated within the **pancreas**.
* **Types:** Described as a **non-α, non-β gastrin-secreting tumor**.
* **Clinical Correlates:** It is a major cause of severe **peptic ulcers** and gastrointestinal complications due to the corrosive effect of sustained high acid levels.
